FightFlow classifies boxing punches (straight, hook, uppercut, none) from video, built as a course project at San Jose State University. Five architectures of increasing complexity train on the same data so the comparison actually means something.

The short answer: pose features beat pixels. A skeleton-based LSTM reaches macro-F1 0.682 while a fine-tuned ResNet-18 stops at 0.570, because pose coordinates stay invariant to background, lighting, and camera angle, which are exactly the things a pixel model memorizes on a 20-video dataset instead of learning punches.

## Results

| Model | Best Epoch | Test Acc | Macro-F1 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline CNN (scratch) | 2 | 0.456 | 0.402 |
| Advanced CNN (residual + attention) | 6 | 0.370 | 0.341 |
| ResNet-18 (ImageNet fine-tune) | 7 | 0.578 | 0.570 |
| CNN+LSTM (ResNet-18 backbone) | 7 | 0.593 | 0.588 |
| **Pose-LSTM (MediaPipe skeleton)** | **31** | **0.692** | **0.682** |

Every number sits on a video-level leakage-free test split of 695 samples. The same models on a naive clip-level split scored as high as 0.933, a reminder that data leakage is silent and flattering.

## Reading the Failures

Three results are worth more than the leaderboard:

- **The Advanced CNN is worse than the Baseline.** More capacity without pretrained weights on 20 videos means the optimizer memorizes training clips rather than building a useful feature hierarchy. That gap is the argument for transfer learning, stated in numbers.
- **CNN+LSTM peaks at the same epoch as ResNet-18.** Both share a ResNet-18 backbone on a 5-epoch freeze schedule, and epoch 7 is the first epoch after unfreeze. The 0.588 against 0.570 margin may come from the backbone getting extra gradient signal through the LSTM loss rather than from real temporal modeling. A frozen-backbone ablation would settle it.
- **Hook is the hardest class** at F1 0.453. A straight punch opens the elbow from roughly 90 to 180 degrees; a hook holds it near 90 throughout while the arm sweeps laterally as a rigid unit. The feature set carries static elbow angles but not elbow angle velocity, and that two-dimensional delta is the direct discriminator.

## Dataset

20 videos yield 3,320 clips across 4 classes, perfectly balanced at 830 samples per class, labeled with a custom frame-level punch annotator. A Group-Aware Stratified Greedy Split partitions at the video level to keep frames from one video out of both halves, producing 2,625 train and 695 test samples in two modalities: RGB frames for the CNN models and 11-frame pose sequences for the Pose-LSTM.

## Pose Feature Vector

The Pose-LSTM runs on 236 engineered dimensions per frame rather than raw landmarks:

| Feature group | Dims | What it captures |
|---|---|---|
| Normalized joint coordinates | 99 | Body pose, hip-centered and scale-normalized |
| Inter-frame velocities | 99 | Joint motion between consecutive frames |
| Relative wrist offsets | 18 | Wrist to shoulder, hip, and nose |
| Peak wrist velocities | 6 | Max x/y/z speed per wrist over the clip |
| Elbow joint angles | 2 | 3D angle at each elbow via law of cosines |
| Wrist-to-nose velocities | 6 | Wrist closing speed toward the face |
| Wrist extension features | 6 | Average velocity in the final 4 frames of extension |

The 11-frame window holds 6 pre-peak frames, the peak, and 4 after. Clip lengths of 8, 10, 11, and 12 were all evaluated; 11 gave the best test F1 and covers the punch arc from wind-up through follow-through.
